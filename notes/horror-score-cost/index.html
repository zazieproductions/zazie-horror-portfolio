How much does a horror film score cost? A 2026 pricing framework

Horror score prices span a wider range than almost any other line in indie film production: the same 20-minute short can be "scored" for $0 with a licensed library pack, or for several thousand dollars as a fully original composition written to the cut. The gap exists because "a score" is four different products. This framework sorts those four bands, shows the per-minute math, and lists the red flags — built on rates actually published by working horror composers, including Zazie Productions' own rate card (micro from $50, shorts from $2,500, features from $8,000, games from $4,500).

IIThe per-minute math (and why features cost less per minute)

Divide a published rate by runtime and a counterintuitive fact appears:

BuildPublished rateRuntimePer-minute
Short score$2,50020 min≈ $125 / min
Feature score$8,00085 min≈ $94 / min

The feature costs less per minute because the expensive work is front-loaded and reusable: spotting logic, theme material, motifs, stems infrastructure, and delivery format. Once a score has a theme system, additional runtime extends it instead of starting over. This is why a short that will become a pilot deserves a bigger score than its length alone suggests — and why you should budget by scope (number of cues, number of themes, revision rounds, stem families), never by minutes alone.

IIIWhat actually moves the number

Within a band, price moves with seven variables. In the order they matter most at indie scale:

  • Runtime and cue count. More picture needs more cues; more cues need more spotting and more stems.
  • Complexity. Adaptive game states, surround or immersive mixes, and hybrid orchestration (real players layered with synthesizers) all cost more than a stereo score of equal length.
  • Revision rounds. Tiers price a fixed number of structured rounds; open-ended "until we love it" revision is what turns a flat fee into a project that runs out of money.
  • Stem families. Delivering 4–8 stems by musical family (the Zazie Productions standard) is more work than a stereo pair, and it is what lets the dub stage do its job.
  • Rush delivery. A deadline in days instead of weeks is a multiplier, not a line item.
  • Live players. Session musicians are a separate budget from composition; "are live players included?" is the most common quoting ambiguity in horror scoring.
  • Rights scope. Full transfer of the score to the production is standard in these tiers; keeping the music for a soundtrack release or album master is a priced add-on, not a surprise.

VRed flags: when "cheap" is misquoted

  • "Custom" music that is a licensed library pack. Ask what percentage of the cues are composed for your film. "All of them" should not be a hard question to answer.
  • No spotting session. A composer who writes before seeing how the music sits in the cut is composing blind; the revisions it takes to fix that are where budgets die.
  • Vague rights. "You own everything" without a cue sheet and a written scope usually means "I own the stems, good luck."
  • No stems, no timecode. A stereo-only handoff cannot survive a re-cut, a re-mix, or a distributor's requirements.
  • One number with no tier breakdown. A real quote shows what is inside the price. A mystery number is a negotiation with you against yourself.

The counter-move is simple: ask for the published rate card before the first call. Zazie Productions publishes its full rate structure — micro from $50, shorts from $2,500, features from $8,000, games from $4,500 — precisely so that the quote conversation starts from numbers instead of vibes.

VIFAQ

01How much does a horror film score cost in 2026?

Horror scoring prices span four bands in 2026: library/sample-based packages for $0–500, micro-budget commissions from around $50 (published micro rates at Zazie Productions start at $50 for naming and student work), full original short-film scores from around $2,500, and feature scores from around $8,000. Game scoring starts around $4,500. The band you land in depends on originality, scope, and delivery, not the genre alone.

02Do horror composer rates scale with film length?

Total price scales with runtime, but per-minute cost usually drops at feature scale. A $2,500 score for a 20-minute short is roughly $125 per minute; an $8,000 score for an 85-minute feature is roughly $94 per minute, because themes, motifs, and spotting infrastructure get reused across a longer cut. Budget by scope (cues, themes, revisions, stems), not by minutes alone.

03Is it worth paying a professional composer for a short horror film?

For festival submission or distribution, yes: original composition is increasingly a requirement rather than a bonus, and a score written to the cut is the cheapest way to make a short feel finished. For a purely private or learning project, a micro-budget tier (from $50 at Zazie Productions) gives a real named composer with a limited cue scope instead of library music.

04What is the difference between a $50 micro score and a $2,500 standard short score?

Scope. The micro tier is a limited build — one to three cues or a naming credit, fewer revision rounds, basic delivery — aimed at student, portfolio, and micro-budget films. The standard tier is a full original score: spotting session, themes and beds written to the cut, stems by family, alternate mixes, timecode-matched handoff, cue sheet, and full rights to the score.
