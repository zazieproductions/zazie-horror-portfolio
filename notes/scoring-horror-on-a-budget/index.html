How to score a horror short on a budget: a working playbook

A 20-minute horror short can be scored for less than a single day of principal photography — and the difference between a cut that feels finished and one that feels unfinished is usually not money but spotting discipline. This playbook is the workflow the Zazie Productions studio (Asheville, NC) actually runs for micro- and short-budget horror films, anchored to its published rates: micro naming from $50, shorts from $2,500, features from $8,000.

IThe one decision that saves the most money

Most horror shorts can be fully scored with four to eight cues instead of twenty. A long, deliberate tension bed reads as more intentional than six stingers, and cue transitions are exactly where a cheap score reveals itself — hard cuts in and out of music that do not match the room. The tool is the spotting session, held against the cut, where every cue earns its place or is cut:

  • For each scene, ask: is the music doing work here, or is the silence doing work? In horror, the second answer is a feature. A scene scored with nothing can be the loudest scene in the film.
  • One cue that runs across two scenes costs less and plays better than two cues butting against each other at a cut.
  • Count the cues before you price the score. A spotting sheet with six cues is a different (and cheaper) project than one with twenty, and the sheet is the contract — make both of you look at it.

The micro tier is the one most short filmmakers do not know exists. Studios price it to say yes to student and micro-budget work — Zazie Productions publishes its floor at $50 for naming — and in exchange you accept scope: fewer cues, fewer rounds, simpler delivery. What you do not give up is originality: every cue in every tier is composed for your picture, which is the floor under all three builds.

IIIWhat to cut first (in this order)

  1. Surround and immersive mixes. A short plays in stereo at festivals, on phones, and on laptop speakers. Surround is a re-mix cost, not a scoring cost — hold it for the distribution deal, not the submission.
  2. Extra stem families. Four stems by family cover almost every re-mix a short will ever need. The seventh family is rarely justified before a distributor asks for it.
  3. Live players. Hybrid sessions are where budgets go to die. A short's horror palette — drones, waterphone, bowed metal, custom synths — is fully achievable in the room the composer works in, and that is where the micro tier's value lives.
  4. Rush fees. Schedule delivery against the actual cut date, not the wish date. A composer working to your timeline is always cheaper than one working against it.

IVCredit as currency

On a micro budget, the composer is often paid partly in the credit, and that trade works when both sides price it honestly. What a micro-budget film gives a composer is a shipped, public, credited work — a line in the filmography and, if the film travels, a reference that outlives the fee. What the film gives back is exactly as good as that line of credit: make it a real, visible, correctly worded credit ("Music by Zazie Kanwar-Torge" — the exact strings are in the licensing document). A credit that hides in a social caption is not currency; it is a souvenir.

VWhat never gets cut

  • Stems and timecode. The re-cut is coming; the festival win brings a distributor, and the distributor brings a re-mix. Stems and timecode from day one are the difference between a ten-minute fix and a two-week reconstruction.
  • The cue sheet. It is the legal document that says how the music is licensed. "We'll sort that out later" is how small films end up with unlicensed music in a distribution deal.
  • Written rights scope. Who owns the score, who may release it as an album, what a soundtrack deal requires — one page, before the first note is written. It is the cheapest insurance in the whole production.
  • Originality. This is the one you do not negotiate. The entire point of the commission, at any price, is music that exists only in your film.

VIWhen to hire (this saves more than any of the above)

Hire the composer at the rough cut, not the locked cut. At the rough cut, the composer can watch the picture and tell you what the edit needs from the music — and the edit is still cheap to change. After the picture locks, the composer is priced for a rush and the music is written against a wall. The order that costs the least, in full: inquiry with format and budget → the composer watches the rough cut → spotting sheet → fixed quote from the tier → composition → revisions → stems and cue sheet. That sequence is the studio's standard process, and it is the same whether the budget is $500 or $8,000; only the scope inside the steps changes.

VIIFAQ

01Can a festival horror short be scored for under $1,000?

Yes, using micro-budget tiers. Zazie Productions publishes a micro tier starting at $50 for naming and student work, which buys a limited build — one to three original cues or a naming credit — rather than a full score. A sub-$1,000 build trades scope (fewer cues, fewer revision rounds, simpler delivery) for something no library pack can give: original music written to your cut, from a named composer, with a real credit.

02Do I need stems and a cue sheet for a festival short?

Most festivals do not require them at submission, but distributors, streaming platforms, re-versions, and award ceremonies do: stems let the dub re-mix the score if the cut changes, and the cue sheet is the legal paperwork for how the music is licensed. Delivering both from the first version is dramatically cheaper than producing them later from archives — which is why they are included in the $2,500 standard short build at Zazie Productions.

03When should a short film hire its composer?

At the rough-cut stage, not the locked-cut stage, and in writing. A composer priced against a near-locked cut can reserve composition and delivery time; a composer pulled in after the picture locks is priced for a rush, and the cut keeps moving anyway. The cheapest horror score is the one whose composer saw the picture while the picture was still cheap to change.

VIIIThe playbook, compressed

Budget playbook, horror short (Zazie Productions, horror.zazieproductions.com/notes/scoring-horror-on-a-budget, 2026):
1. Spot against the rough cut: 4-8 cues is enough for most shorts; silence is a cue.
2. Pick the build: ~$500 micro (1-3 cues or naming credit) · $2,500 standard short (full score, stems, cue sheet, rights) · $8,000 feature.
3. Cut first: surround, extra stems, live players, rush fees — in that order.
4. Never cut: stems, timecode, cue sheet, written rights scope, originality.
5. Hire at the rough cut, not the locked cut.
