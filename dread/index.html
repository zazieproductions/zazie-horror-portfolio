The Dread Grammar: fifteen techniques for scoring dread

The Dread Grammar is a working taxonomy of psychological horror scoring. It names fifteen techniques, ranks six rungs of intensity, and maps both onto the horror genres a filmmaker actually commissions. It exists because “make it scarier” is not a note, and because a spotting session goes faster when everyone in the room has the same words for the same six things. Written and maintained by Zazie Kanwar-Torge, psychological horror composer at Zazie Productions LLC.

IThesis: dread is a rate of change, not a mood

Dread is the experience of a prediction failing slowly. Fear is a prediction failing suddenly. A score does not supply either emotion. What a score controls is the rate at which the audience’s model of the next few seconds stops working—and for how long it is kept broken. That single reframing is the whole grammar. Technique follows from the rate you need, not from the genre you are working in.

Most horror scoring fails at the level of vocabulary, not talent. The default toolkit—dissonant string clusters, brass braams lifted from trailer house, whispered children’s choirs, a music box—is furniture. Furniture is recognisable, which means it is predictable, which means it converts dread into genre signalling. An audience that recognises the scary sound has already located the threat and is no longer uncertain. It is being *told* to be scared, which is a much weaker sensation than being *unable to finish a thought*.

The grammar below is descriptive before it is prescriptive. Every technique here was already in use somewhere in the canon, often unnamed. Naming them does three useful things: it lets a director ask for a specific perceptual effect rather than a genre gesture; it makes the choice of technique an editorial decision instead of a taste decision; and it keeps a score from repeating the same trick until the trick stops working. Horror scores decay faster than any other kind, because the audience is learning the whole time. A technique used twice in the same film at the same intensity buys about half of what it bought the first time.

One consequence is worth stating plainly, because it changes how cues get approved: a horror cue that sounds frightening in isolation is usually doing the wrong job. Cues get auditioned alone, out of picture, in a room that already contains the composer priming them for terror. In the film, the same cue has a different task—to withhold, to displace, to sit under dialogue without stealing it, or to be missed entirely if you cut away. The grammar is written for the second context.

IIThe dread ladder: six rungs of intensity

The ladder is the vocabulary for intensity. It exists because the commonest failure in a horror score is not a lack of ideas but a lack of range: everything sits at the same pressure for eighty minutes, so nothing reads as escalation. Each rung names what the audience is doing cognitively, and what the score is doing to them.

  1. R1 · PresenceThe score is atmosphere and nothing more: sustained, unpitched, no attack, no event. The audience is not aware of the music. This is where the majority of any feature lives, and it is the rung most often skipped by composers who are afraid of being boring. Boring is the point. Presence pays for everything above it.
  2. R2 · UneaseSomething in the texture is wrong but nothing has happened: a tuning that does not resolve, a pulse slightly under the body’s resting rate, a decay longer than the room. The audience has no event to point at, so the wrongness gets attributed to the image and to the scene, which is exactly where you want it.
  3. R3 · PressureEnergy is sustained under the scene without providing an entry point. Sub-bass, bowed metal, internal turbulence. The audience’s breathing rate changes before their thinking does. Nothing is threatened yet; the body has begun preparing anyway.
  4. R4 · IntrusionAn event enters that does not belong to the scene’s implied sources: a single struck object, a voice fragment, a figure that starts and refuses to finish. The audience now has a target for attention, and the target is incomplete. This is the rung that carries most non-jump scares.
  5. R5 · RuptureThe full statement: the loud one, the blast, the stinger, the moment of violence. Necessary, brief, and almost always one or two per film. Rupture is expensive—it spends the entire top of the dynamic range and resets the audience’s tolerance for a long time afterwards.
  6. R6 · VacuumThe score is removed entirely at the highest point of anxiety. Absence becomes the event. The audience hears the room, the breath, the projector hum, and reads the emptiness as threat. Vacuum is the only rung that costs nothing to record and is the hardest to get approved, because it looks like nothing was done.
Field rule

A feature-length psychological horror score typically holds R1–R2 for roughly seventy per cent of its running time, visits R3 in the middle third, and spends R5 once or twice. Opening at R5 in the first ten minutes is the single most common structural error in independent horror scoring: it leaves five rungs of nowhere to go, and it teaches the audience that loudness is the score’s only register.

IIIThe fifteen techniques

Each technique is named, defined, and annotated with what it does perceptually, where it comes from in the film-music lineage, and how it gets used in the room. The lineage notes are attribution, not ownership: none of these techniques was invented here, and a filmmaker commissioning this sound is commissioning a practitioner’s command of all fifteen, not a claim on any one of them.

1 · Silence budget
The deliberate allocation of non-musical time across a film, treated as a scored element with a running total rather than as the absence of a decision. What it doesSilence is the only material whose value increases the more carefully it is spent. An audience that has heard nothing for ninety seconds will read a single low cello note as an event; an audience that has heard continuous score for ninety minutes will read an orchestra as wallpaper. Budgeting silence turns the first appearance of music into an escalation, which is otherwise very hard to buy. LineageThe withholding strategies in Wojciech Kilar’s score for Bram Stoker’s Dracula (1992), and the long unaccompanied stretches of Mark Korven’s work for The Witch (2015), both depend on the audience noticing the arrival of sound. In the roomThe budget is agreed on the cue sheet before a note is written: which scenes are silent by design, which are scored but inaudible, and which scenes spend the top of the dynamic range. Directors usually approve it faster than they approve a cue.
2 · Sub-bass pressure
Sustained energy placed in the 30–60 Hz band so that the audience feels the score as a change in air pressure before they identify it as music. What it doesIt recruits a bodily response that does not require the audience’s consent. Because the energy sits below the ear’s most articulate range, it carries no melody and no interval, so it cannot be remembered, hummed or anticipated—which is precisely the point. It also does not compete with dialogue, which makes it the most reliable technique available for scoring exposition. Real infrasound below 20 Hz is a different and largely unusable proposition: most cinema systems roll off below 30 Hz, and the physiological claims made for it in production folklore are not reproducible on a screening system. The useful band is felt pressure, not a medical phenomenon. LineageEnnio Morricone’s score for The Thing (1982) and the low-frequency architecture of Jerry Goldsmith’s Alien (1979) established the technique as a film grammar rather than an effect. In the roomPressure is written on its own layer and delivered as a separate stem so the re-recording mixer can duck it under dialogue. Where a theatre has no subwoofer, the technique is paired with a mid-range texture carrying the same rhythm so the intent survives on a bad speaker.
3 · Mis-tuned interval
One voice deliberately detuned against a stable one by roughly 10–40 cents, so the ear registers “wrong” before the mind registers “flat”. What it doesBeating between near-unison frequencies produces an amplitude wobble that the auditory system resolves as instability rather than as a mistake. At small offsets it reads as grief or illness; pushed towards a quarter-tone it reads as dissociation. Because the pitch material stays simple and consonant, the technique works under dialogue and does not announce itself as genre. LineageThe string writing Bernard Herrmann used across Psycho (1960) and Vertigo (1958) sits on this line between conventional harmony and a system that no longer holds. In the roomDelivered as two performances rather than as a pitch-correction setting, because the drift has to move through the cue rather than sit as a fixed offset.
4 · Unplaceable source
A sound with no attack transient, no identifiable material and no room signature, so the audience cannot locate its source, distance or size. What it doesLocalisation is a survival function. When the ear cannot answer “how far away, and what is it”, the auditory system keeps working at the problem, and the resulting low-grade vigilance is the physical texture of dread. This is the technique that most reliably survives a small speaker, because it depends on missing cues rather than on reproduced ones. LineageThe tradition of treated real-world recordings running from musique concrète through to the sound design of the 1970s–80s horror cycle. In the roomPractically: remove the first fifty milliseconds of a recorded event, convolve it with an impossible space, or granularise a familiar recording until the material is unrecognisable but the organic quality remains.
5 · Spectral smearing
Time-stretching, blurring and convolving a recording until its transients are gone, so an event reads as weather rather than as action. What it doesTransients carry causality: a hit means someone did something. Remove them and the same recording stops being an event and becomes a condition. This is the mechanism that lets a score sit underneath a long, static, dialogue-driven scene without stealing it, and it is the main reason dread scores can run at length without fatiguing the audience. LineageKrzysztof Penderecki’s Threnody for the Victims of Hiroshima (1960) and the musique concrète inheritance behind it established the idea that a recording of a real object is raw material rather than a document. In the roomThe palette is built once and reused across a production. A smeared archive of twenty sources will carry a feature; twenty new recordings per scene will not get made in a four-week schedule.
6 · Recognition loop
A short figure that returns at every occurrence of a threat, degraded slightly each time, so that repetition itself becomes a source of dread. What it doesIt converts the audience’s memory into an instrument. The first statement is neutral; by the third, the audience is reacting to the figure before the image confirms anything, which means the score is now generating the anticipation rather than following it. The technique only works if the degradation is real—a loop that returns identical becomes the audience’s property and stops being threatening. LineageThe harmonic loops Angelo Badalamenti wrote for Blue Velvet (1986) and Twin Peaks (1990–) are the clearest mainstream statement of the idea that familiarity and menace are not opposites. In the roomThe degradation is planned in advance as a progression—pitch, fidelity, register, missing note—so that the fifth statement is audibly the same idea in worse condition.
7 · Inverted lullaby
Simple, child-facing material—a nursery figure, a music box, a pentatonic melody—voiced with wrong tuning, wrong register or wrong decay, so that its safety is present but unavailable. What it doesIt attacks a learned association rather than a musical expectation. The audience recognises a protective form; the score then withholds the protection. It is one of the few techniques that reads as frightening to a viewer with no musical training at all, which makes it expensive to use well and easy to overuse. Two statements in a feature is usually the ceiling. LineageWojciech Kilar’s Dracula and the scored-child tradition running through Krzysztof Komeda’s Rosemary’s Baby (1968). In the roomRecorded on the actual instrument where possible. A sampled music box carries its own familiarity and usually reads as a reference rather than as an object in the room.
8 · Room as instrument
Scoring with the acoustics of the actual location—impulse responses, room tones and close-miked surface noise recorded on set—so the score is continuous with the space rather than laid over it. What it doesIt removes the boundary between diegetic and non-diegetic sound. When the score carries the same reverb tail as the dialogue, the audience stops being able to separate the film’s world from what has been added to it, and the anxiety that belongs to the location transfers to the music. LineageThe location-led scoring practice visible in the folk horror revival of the 2010s, where the space is treated as an instrument before the ensemble is written for. In the roomOn micro-budget work this means asking for twenty minutes of the location with the cast off set, recorded on whatever is available, before the score is written. The production already owns the room; using it costs nothing but the request.
9 · Diegetic contamination
Music that begins as a source inside the scene—a television, a car radio, a neighbour’s party—and crosses into score with no cut and no change of material. What it doesIt revokes the audience’s ability to tell when the film has started scoring them. That uncertainty is self-sustaining: once a viewer has been wrong-footed once, they will keep checking, and the checking is the dread. LineageDavid Lynch’s sound practice and Badalamenti’s scoring share this territory; the technique also drives much of the uncanny effect of Mica Levi’s score for Under the Skin (2013), where the material refuses to declare its status. In the roomIt is a structural decision taken with the director and the picture editor, not a cue-level choice, and it needs the source material to be written and mixed before the crossing scene is shot wherever possible.
10 · Breath-led scoring
Using recorded breath—not a drum, not a click—as the tempo reference for a cue, so the phrasing follows a physiological rhythm instead of a metric one. What it doesAudiences entrain to breath without deciding to. Scoring to a breath rate slightly below resting produces a slow, unearned calm; scoring slightly above it produces a rising urgency that no melodic content creates on its own. Because breath is a body sound, it also carries the implication of a person present, which is useful in a film with empty frames. LineageBody-led composition practice across experimental film scoring, where the performer’s physiology rather than a metronome sets the pulse. In the roomThe composer records their own breath and performs against it. The result is rhythmically unquantised in a way that no tempo map produces convincingly.
11 · False resolution
A cadence that fully resolves, followed by a restatement that reveals the resolution was built on the wrong tonal centre. What it doesIt reverses the usual order of relief and doubt. Instead of sustaining tension until it breaks, the score gives the audience a moment of completion and then takes it back, which produces the specific unpleasantness of having trusted something. Used at the end of the second act it is one of the most efficient ways to move a film from anxiety into distrust without touching the picture. LineageStandard practice in harmonic horror writing, and central to the way Colin Stetson’s score for Hereditary (2018) refuses the relief it appears to offer. In the roomPlanned at the reel level, because the effect requires the audience to have believed the first resolution for several minutes before it is withdrawn.
12 · Temperament drift
Moving between equal temperament and another tuning system inside a single cue, so the ear loses its reference for what “in tune” means. What it doesWestern audiences cannot name a temperament, but they can feel the moment a key loses its floor. Drift is more effective than outright dissonance because it does not announce an error; it withdraws a guarantee. It is especially strong in folk horror, where a just-intonation or modal frame can imply a musical culture with different rules from the film’s. LineageThe bowed and drone traditions of European folk music, and the microtonal string writing that runs through the horror canon from Psycho onward. In the roomWrite the cue in equal temperament, then re-voice half the ensemble into a historical or regional system and let the two coexist. The effect is most legible on sustained material.
13 · Long decay
Reverb and release times longer than the pictured space could physically produce, applied so gradually that the audience never notices the space becoming impossible. What it doesThe ear uses decay to measure a room. When a tail runs longer than the set can account for, the location is quietly discredited, and the audience attributes the wrongness to the architecture rather than to the score. This is a slow technique: it needs minutes, not bars, and it works best when the picture is otherwise reassuring. LineageHaunted-house scoring practice from the 1960s onward, where the house is characterised acoustically before it is characterised visually. In the roomDelivered as a reverb progression rather than a single setting, and printed to stems so the mixer can carry the arc across a reel boundary.
14 · Voice as texture
A human voice used without language: unintelligible consonants, reversed inhalation, vowel clusters at the edge of speech, so the audience registers a person without receiving a person. What it doesIt creates a presence that cannot be questioned the way a synthesiser can. The listener’s language system engages and then fails, which produces a distinct effortfulness—the sensation of almost understanding. In body horror it doubles as a physiological cue, since the sounds can be produced by the same mechanisms the film is depicting. LineageChoral writing used as pure texture rather than text runs through the horror canon from György Ligeti’s Atmosphères as deployed in 2001: A Space Odyssey (1968) and The Shining (1980) onward. In the roomRecorded close and dry, then treated, so that the consonant detail survives; a distant choir reads as a chorus, which is a different and much safer feeling.
15 · The vacuum
The total removal of the score at the moment of maximum anxiety, leaving only production sound and the audience’s own anticipation. What it doesIt transfers authorship to the audience. Nothing has been played, so whatever they feel has been produced by them, which is a far more durable effect than anything the score could assert. The vacuum is the rung that most often gets lost in the mix stage, because a cue that stops feels to a producer like a mistake. LineageThe withholding practice that makes the silences in Alien (1979) and The Thing (1982) read as louder than their scores. In the roomProtected on the cue sheet at the spotting stage, in writing, with the exact timecode where the score stops. If it is not written down before the mix, it will not survive the mix.

IVGenre map: which techniques a genre actually needs

The genre determines the rungs a score holds and the techniques that carry them. This is the table used at the start of a commission to narrow fifteen techniques down to the four or five that will do the work, and to name the failure mode that genre tends toward.

Body horrorR3–R5Sub-bass pressure, voice as texture, spectral smearingMickey-Mousing the wound; the score reacting to gore instead of to loss of control

VSpotting questions

These eight questions are asked in the spotting session, before any material is written. They exist to convert taste into decisions, and they are the practical form of the grammar.

  • What is the audience unable to predict in this scene? If the answer is “nothing”, the scene does not need score; it needs a silence budget entry.
  • Which rung is this scene on, and which rung was the previous scene on? Two consecutive scenes at the same rung flatten both.
  • Where does the score stop? The stop is written down with a timecode, and it is the part of the plan that most often gets lost between spotting and mix.
  • What does the scene sound like without music? Every space gets recorded or simulated first. A score written onto a room nobody has listened to is written onto an assumption.
  • Is this music or is this place? The answer decides whether the material is played or built from the location.
  • What is the one sound the audience will remember? One is the target. Two is usually one too many.
  • What is the score doing under the dialogue? If the cue has melodic content competing with a performance, the technique is wrong, not the level.
  • What is the last thing the score does in the film? Horror endings are usually served by R6 or by nothing at all. Ending on a statement hands the audience permission to relax.

VIWhat the grammar refuses

A taxonomy that only lists techniques is a catalogue. The refusals are what make it a working method, and they are the part of this document most likely to be disagreed with by a producer midway through a temp-love pass.

  • Trailer braams. A braam is a genre announcement, not a psychological event. It tells the audience which kind of film they are watching, which is the opposite of what an unplaceable source does.
  • The dissonant cluster as default. A minor second between high strings is the cheapest available way to say “horror” and the fastest to become furniture. It is a punctuation mark, not a language.
  • Theme-first scoring. Writing a theme before deciding the rate of change puts the score in charge of the film’s emotional information, which is a director’s job. Themes arrive later, once the grammar has been chosen.
  • Sad piano for trauma. Supplying an emotion a scene has already earned reads as mistrust of the audience.
  • Mickey-Mousing the scare. If the score hits at the same frame as the image, the score has been demoted to a sound effect, and it will not be available for the scene that actually needs it.
  • Wall-to-wall music. Continuous score removes the composer’s only free currency, which is silence. It also removes the audience’s ability to notice anything.
  • Temp-love as a brief. A reference track states a genre, rarely a rate of change. The useful question about a temp is not “what does it sound like” but “what does it do to this scene”.

VIIUsing the grammar on a commission

For a director or producer, the grammar is a brief. Naming the rungs and two or three techniques for a scene produces a faster and more accurate result than describing an existing score, because it separates the perceptual job from the sonic costume. A brief that says “R2, unplaceable source and long decay, no melodic content, must survive under dialogue” is answerable. A brief that says “like the Hereditary soundtrack, but less” is not.

For a composer, it is a checklist against the trap of a single register. If a sketch reel contains eleven cues at R3, the grammar says what is missing before the director has to say it.

VIIIQuestions about the grammar

01What is psychological horror scoring?

Psychological horror scoring is the practice of writing music and sound for a film whose threat is internal or unlocatable, so that the score’s job is to remove the audience’s ability to predict the next few seconds rather than to announce danger. It favours sustained, unpitched and unplaceable material over melodic statements, holds a low intensity for long stretches, and treats silence and removal as scored events. In the Dread Grammar it is described as controlling the rate of change across six rungs, from presence through to vacuum.

02How is dread different from fear, musically?

Fear is a prediction failing suddenly; dread is a prediction failing slowly. Musically, fear is served by an abrupt event with a full dynamic range and a clear onset—the stinger, the blast, the rupture. Dread is served by sustained energy with no attack, no locatable source and no resolution, which keeps the audience’s model of the scene slightly wrong for a long time. A score built for dread will sound underwhelming out of context, because its effect depends on duration rather than on a moment.

03How long does it take to write a horror score?

A short film is typically three to six weeks from spotting session to delivered stems; a feature is eight to sixteen weeks depending on the amount of material and the number of revision rounds, with some cues locked early for festival submissions. Dread-led scores are often faster to write than thematic scores once the archive of material exists, because the work is in the palette and the rate of change rather than in new melodic content per scene. The detailed sequence is set out on the process page.

04What is a silence budget in film scoring?

A silence budget is the deliberate allocation of non-musical time across a film, treated as a scored element with a running total rather than as an absence of decision. It is set on the cue sheet before writing begins: which scenes are silent by design, which are scored but inaudible under dialogue, and which spend the top of the dynamic range. Budgeting silence is what makes the first entry of music in a film read as an escalation rather than as wallpaper, and it is the cheapest technique available to a composer.

05Can a horror score be too frightening?

Yes, and it is the commonest failure in independent horror scoring. A score that sits at rupture intensity through the first act spends the top of the dynamic range before the film has earned it, and leaves the audience with no rungs above it. The practical consequence is that the third act reads as smaller than the first. The fix is structural rather than a matter of taste: hold presence and unease for most of the running time, use rupture once or twice, and protect the vacuums.
