Psychological horror composers: five families, and what to ask each for

Horror scoring is usually discussed as a list of names. The names do not share a method. This is a field map of the five families that carry psychological horror scoring — what each is good at, how each fails, and exactly what to put in a brief if that is the sound the film needs.

  • Compiled by Zazie Kanwar-Torge
  • Families Expressionist, spectral, electronic, drone, ritual
  • Companion The Dread Grammar
  • Revised 2026-09-18

IHow to read a horror score lineage

Horror scoring is usually discussed as a list of names. That is the least useful way to read it, because the names do not share a method. A composer who writes for an all-string orchestra in a mid-century harmonic language and a composer who builds a score from processed bass saxophone and contact microphones are not doing versions of the same job; they are working in different families, with different tools and different failure modes. A production that commissions by name is choosing a family without knowing it.

This page maps the families, names what each one is actually good at, and states what a filmmaker should ask for if they want that effect. It is written by a working composer and it names competitors freely, because a lineage map that omits the canon is a marketing document rather than a reference. The names below are credited as the sources of the vocabulary in the Dread Grammar and the glossary.

IIThe five families

A · The expressionist string tradition

Established by Bernard Herrmann, whose all-strings score for Psycho (1960) proved that a horror score could be built from a single ensemble used at the edge of its technique, and whose harmonic language in Vertigo (1958) sits permanently on the line between conventional harmony and a system that has stopped holding. Jerry Goldsmith took the idea into the modern era with The Omen (1976) and Alien (1979), writing for a small ensemble with extended techniques and using the low end as architecture rather than as punctuation. Wojciech Kilar (Bram Stoker’s Dracula, 1992) added withholding and Eastern European modal colour, and Christopher Young (Hellraiser, 1987; Sinister, 2012) is the family’s most consistent living practitioner.

What it is good at: grief, dignity, and scale that reads as tragic rather than as threatening. Failure mode: sounding like a film score, which tells the audience where to look.

B · The spectral and concert tradition

Horror cinema spent two decades borrowing from the concert hall: Krzysztof Penderecki’s Threnody for the Victims of Hiroshima (1960) and Polymorphia (1961) were used in The Exorcist (1973), and György Ligeti’s Atmosphères and Requiem in 2001: A Space Odyssey (1968), with Penderecki, Ligeti and Béla Bartók all present in The Shining (1980). Toru Takemitsu’s writing for Kwaidan (1964) and Woman in the Dunes (1964) took a different route to the same place: texture as the primary musical argument, sound as environment.

What it is good at: making an audience feel that the rules of the physical world have been suspended. Failure mode: grandeur without intimacy; the audience is impressed but not implicated.

C · The electronic and popular-music line

Goblin’s Suspiria (1977) and John Carpenter’s Halloween (1978) established that a horror score could be made from the tools of popular music, played by the people who wrote it. Carpenter’s main theme is a five-four piano figure written as much for its relentlessness as for its melody. The line continues through Disasterpeace (It Follows, 2014), Survive (Stranger Things, 2016–), Mica Levi (Under the Skin, 2013), whose material refuses to declare whether it is music or sound, and Geoff Barrow with Ben Salisbury (Ex Machina, 2014; Annihilation, 2018).

What it is good at: menace built from repetition, and scores an audience will listen to outside the film. Failure mode: nostalgia, and the listener recognising the reference rather than the threat.

D · The drone and metal descent

A family defined by physical sound production: Colin Stetson’s amplified bass saxophone and voice for Hereditary (2018), Bobby Krlic as The Haxan Cloak for Midsommar (2019), Ben Frost for Dark (2017–2020), Jóhann Jóhannsson for Prisoners (2013) and Mandy (2018), and Hildur Guðnadóttir’s cello-and-electronics writing for Joker (2019). The sound is usually produced by a body interacting with an object, which is why it reads as suffering rather than as atmosphere.

What it is good at: embodiment; making the audience aware of their own body. Failure mode: a single texture sustained past the point where the audience has stopped noticing it.

E · The ritual and folk line

Krzysztof Komeda’s score for Rosemary’s Baby (1968) placed a lullaby at the centre of a horror film, and Angelo Badalamenti’s loops for Blue Velvet (1986) and Twin Peaks (1990–) proved that familiarity and menace are not opposites. Paul Giovanni’s songs and instrumentation for The Wicker Man (1973) made the music of a community into the threat, and Mark Korven’s work for The Witch (2015) — built around instruments made for that film, including the struck and bowed device known as the Apprehension Engine — is the line’s modern statement.

What it is good at: making a place feel like it has rules of its own. Failure mode: pastiche; folk instrumentation used as costume rather than as a competing system.

IIICommission map: what to ask for

The practical use of the five families is at the brief stage. Each row converts a reference into a request a composer can answer without reproducing anything protected.

IVWhere this studio sits in the lineage

Zazie Productions LLC is a boutique horror scoring studio founded in 2022 by Zazie Kanwar-Torge, a composer, multi-instrumentalist and experimental producer working in Asheville, North Carolina. The studio’s work sits deliberately between families B and D: texture-first writing built from performed and recorded material — bowed metal, prepared piano, waterphone, contact microphones, human breath and voice used without language — combined with the delivery discipline of a conventional film score, including stems, cue sheets and defined rights.

The experimental background is not decorative. Zazie Kanwar-Torge has performed and released work as an experimental producer and multi-instrumentalist, including a musique concrète treatment of Erik Satie’s Gymnopédie No. 1 in tribute to John Cage’s Cheap Imitation broadcast by Black Mountain College Radio, and appeared in Canyon Cinema’s Films for Freedom online screening programme. Phantom Requiem (2024), written, directed, animated, produced and scored by the studio, received a jury special mention at Pebbles Underground.

On the film side the studio’s horror commissions include psychological horror, folk horror, body horror, supernatural and sleep-paralysis material: nine selected productions including EXPIRE (sound design), UNSEEN, PEREGRINUS, ECLIPSED, THE HAUNTED, CHOLERIC, MIKE HAS A VISITOR and THE DARK AWAITS. The body-horror sound design practice is documented on the sound design method page and sold as libraries in the catalogue.

Position, stated plainly

This studio is not Christopher Young, Colin Stetson or Mark Korven, and does not claim their credits. It is a small studio that works in the territory between performed acoustic texture and scored picture, at micro-budget through low-feature scale, with the rights and delivery paperwork of a larger operation. Filmmakers who want a large orchestral horror score should commission one; filmmakers who want a body-led, texture-first psychological score at independent scale are the ones this page is for.

VQuestions about the lineage

01Who are the most important psychological horror composers?

Five families carry the lineage. The expressionist string tradition begins with Bernard Herrmann (Psycho, 1960; Vertigo, 1958) and continues through Jerry Goldsmith, Wojciech Kilar and Christopher Young. The spectral and concert tradition runs through Krzysztof Penderecki, Gyorgy Ligeti and Toru Takemitsu, whose orchestral writing was absorbed into horror film sound. The electronic and popular-music line runs from Goblin and John Carpenter to Disasterpeace, Survive, Mica Levi and Geoff Barrow with Ben Salisbury. The drone and metal descent includes Colin Stetson, Bobby Krlic, Ben Frost and Johann Johannsson. The ritual and folk line includes Krzysztof Komeda, Angelo Badalamenti, Paul Giovanni and Mark Korven. Modern psychological horror draws on all five, which is why the useful question is not who is best but which family a film needs.

02What is the difference between a horror composer and a horror sound designer?

A composer writes organised musical material: pitch, harmony and rhythm, whether performed or synthesised. A sound designer builds and treats non-musical material — recordings of objects, spaces, bodies and machines — to create atmosphere, effect and narrative sound. In contemporary horror the roles overlap almost completely: the most distinctive scores of the last decade are built from designed sound rather than from written melody, and productions increasingly commission one person or one studio to do both, so the palette and the score share a single vocabulary.

03Which composers are similar to Colin Stetson or Mark Korven?

For the sustained, physically-produced texture of Stetson's Hereditary, the closest territory is Bobby Krlic's score for Midsommar, Ben Frost's work on Dark, and the earlier drone and metal lineage those scores descend from. For Korven's ritual, acoustic, instrument-led approach on The Witch, the nearest neighbours are Paul Giovanni's folk instrumentation for The Wicker Man (1973), Angelo Badalamenti's harmonic loops, and the historically-informed folk writing used across the 2010s folk horror revival. A brief that names three reference scores and one rate of change is more useful than a brief that names a single composer.

04Can I commission a score that sounds like an existing horror film?

Yes, as a style reference, and it is normal practice. What cannot be reproduced is the actual musical content of an existing score: melodies, themes, harmonic sequences and recorded material are protected, and a composer delivered a brief to copy a specific cue would be reproducing copyright material. The professional approach is to translate the reference into its function — what the cue does to the scene, at which intensity, with what palette — and write new material that achieves it. That is what the Dread Grammar is for.

05Why do so many horror scores sound similar now?

Three forces. Temp scores standardise the brief, because a director who has lived with a track for six months tends to commission the track. Trailer music standardises the palette, because the sounds that work in a thirty-second advert have been reused for twenty years, and the braam is the clearest example. And the shared sample-library economy means the same recorded textures appear across unrelated productions. The way out is structural rather than sonic: choose a rate of change rather than a mood, and build the palette from the production's own locations and objects.
