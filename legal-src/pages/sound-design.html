Unsettling sound design: subtraction as a method

Horror sound design is usually described by its outputs. The method here is subtraction: record the physical world, remove everything that identifies a sound, and keep what a listener can feel but cannot name. This page publishes the material list, the five passes, and the library behind the studio’s body horror work.

  • Practice lead Zazie Kanwar-Torge
  • Palette Bowed metal, body, architecture, impulse responses
  • Library 209 body horror sources
  • Revised 2026-09-18

IWhat this studio means by unsettling sound design

Sound design in horror is normally described by its outputs: creature vocals, gore, whooshes, atmospheres. That is a catalogue, not a method. The method here is subtraction. Material is recorded from the physical world because bodies and objects carry an authenticity that synthesis does not, and then its identifying features are removed one at a time — the transient that gives it causality, the room that gives it a size, the formant that gives it a species, the fidelity that gives it a date — until what remains is a sound the listener can feel but cannot name.

That unresolvable quality is the product. A sound an audience can identify is information; a sound they cannot identify is a problem their nervous system keeps trying to solve, and the duration of that attempt is dread. Everything on this page serves that one behaviour, and the vocabulary used to describe it is defined in the glossary.

The same practice is sold as libraries: the body horror and gore collection in the catalogue is 209 designed sources in ten labelled categories, built from the same recordings that appear in commissioned work. A library cannot deliver a film’s palette, but it can deliver a production’s effects floor.

IIThe material list

Fourteen categories of material, with what each one is for. This list is published because a director who knows what a bowed cymbal does for a scene can brief it in a sentence, and because a composer who cannot explain their palette is usually relying on one.

Bowed metal
Cymbals, saw blades, gongs, springs and coiled cable excited with a bow rather than a striker. Supplies sustained, pitch-unstable tone with no attack transient, which is the single most useful behaviour in unsettling sound design. See unplaceable source.
Contact-miked architecture
Handrails, pipes, doors, floorboards and stairwells recorded through a transducer attached to the structure itself. Captures sound from inside the building, so the material carries the room’s physical reality rather than a microphone’s perspective on it.
Prepared piano
A piano with objects placed on and between its strings, so the decay, pitch and mechanical noise of the instrument are altered. Reads as a domestic object behaving incorrectly, which is the exact quality supernatural and psychological horror need.
Waterphone
A steel bowl with tuned rods and a water chamber, bowed or struck. Produces a shimmering, unplaceable tone with an unusually long tail, and it is the reason the sound has been a supernatural-horror standard since the 1970s.
Body-sourced foley
Recorded breath, mouth, skin, joint and throat sounds performed to picture. The physical proximity of the recording is the point: a listener recognises the sound of a body, whether or not they can name what the body is doing.
Voice without language
Human vocal material used as texture: unintelligible consonants, reversed inhalation, vowel clusters at the edge of speech. The language system engages and fails, producing the effortfulness of almost understanding. See voice as texture.
Field recordings
Unprocessed recordings of real places — substations, tunnels, quarries, hospitals, empty pools — used as beds or as source material for processed layers. Every production’s own locations are recorded where the schedule allows.
Impulse-response capture
Measuring how a specific space responds to a sound, then using that measurement as the reverb for the score. The location and the music then decay together and the audience loses the boundary between them. See room as instrument.
Transient removal
Deleting or blurring the first tens of milliseconds of an event so causality disappears and the sound becomes a condition rather than an action. See spectral smearing.
Granular processing
Rebuilding a recording from thousands of very short fragments so time, pitch and texture can be controlled independently, producing material that is recognisably organic but no longer identifiable.
Sub-harmonic generation
Building a pitch an octave or two below a source so it is felt as pressure rather than heard as pitch, then delivering it as a separate stem for the re-recording mixer. See sub-bass pressure.
Damage and degradation
Tape saturation, wow and flutter, bit reduction and dropouts applied until a clean recording carries the unreliability of a found object. Used sparingly: damage is a signifier, and a signifier that appears in every cue stops signifying.
Designed silence
Removing a layer at the moment of maximum information, so the audience hears the room instead of the score. The cheapest and most frequently lost technique in the whole practice. See the vacuum.
Spatial placement
Positioning material around and behind the audience in immersive and surround formats so a threat occupies a location the picture does not show. See ambisonics.

IIIThe five passes

  1. CaptureLocation recording, instrument and object sessions, body sessions, and impulse-response measurement of the film’s own spaces. Nothing is designed before the raw material exists, because a palette invented at the desk sounds like a palette invented at the desk.
  2. DestroyEach source is processed until it has lost the feature that identifies it, and then checked: can a listener still name it? If yes, the process continues. This pass produces the film’s vocabulary rather than its cues.
  3. PlaceMaterial is positioned in the space the image has established, using the location’s own impulse response where one exists. The rule is that the audience should never be able to prove the sound is not coming from the room.
  4. PerformDesigned sounds are performed to picture rather than pasted onto it, so their timing follows the cut and the performance instead of a grid. This is where sound design becomes scoring: a breath, a struck object or a bowed tail can carry the rhythmic role a percussion part would have taken.
  5. DeliverStems, M&E, alternates, cue sheets and a written statement of rights. The delivery standard is the same for a $50 micro-budget commission and a feature, because a production that cannot re-version its film later is a production that has been badly served.

IVThe body horror library

The collection sold in the catalogue is 209 designed sources in ten labelled categories — bone breaks, flesh tearing, wet impacts, creature vocals, breath and throat, joints and cartilage, sub-pressure hits, tails and risers, degraded textures and room beds — delivered as 24-bit WAV with metadata and a written licence. It was built for the studio’s own body horror commissions, which is why it reflects that genre’s requirements rather than a general-purpose effects catalogue.

Two notes for anyone buying a horror library. First, a designed sound is only half a sound: what sells an impact in the mix is the tail, so every source here ships with its layers separated. Second, check the licence rather than the folder size. A library that cannot be used in a broadcast production is not a tool, and the licence terms in this studio’s licensing document state exactly what a production may do with the material.

VQuestions about sound design

01What is unsettling sound design?

Unsettling sound design is the construction of atmosphere and effect from non-musical recorded material — objects, spaces, bodies, structures — with the specific aim of making an audience perceive a threat without being able to locate or identify it. It works by removal rather than addition: transients are deleted so a hit stops being an action, sources are disguised so they cannot be recognised, and decays are stretched past what the pictured space allows. The result is a sound the listening brain keeps working at, and that unresolved effort is the physical texture of dread.

02Do I need both a composer and a sound designer?

Usually one person or one studio can do both on independent productions, and doing both is often better: the palette and the score then share a single vocabulary, and a cue can cross from designed sound into score without a seam. Larger productions sometimes split the roles, in which case the useful division is that the sound designer owns the film’s non-musical reality and the composer owns the rate of change. See the lineage page for how the two roles now overlap.

03Can you design sound for a film you are not scoring?

Yes. Sound design commissions are taken separately from scoring commissions, including creature and gore design, atmosphere beds, trailer and title design, and the design of a single signature sound for a production. The same delivery standards apply: sessions or source files as agreed, stems, and a written statement of what the production owns.

04How long does sound design take?

A short film’s atmospheric design is typically two to four weeks, depending on how much of the film is designed rather than cut from library material. Feature work is scheduled in passes alongside the edit. Commissioning the design before picture lock is strongly preferred, because the film’s own locations can be recorded and used as the score’s acoustics, which is a technique that cannot be retrofitted afterwards.

05Can we record our own location and have you use it?

That is the recommended route where a schedule allows it. Twenty minutes of the main location with cast and crew off set, recorded on any available device, gives the studio room tone, surface detail and an acoustic measurement of the space. The production already owns the room; using it as the score’s reverb costs nothing beyond the request.
