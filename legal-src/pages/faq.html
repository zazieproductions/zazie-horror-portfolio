I. Commissioning

What the studio scores, who it works with, and how a project starts.

01What kind of projects do you score?

Original scores for psychological horror, folk horror, body horror, supernatural thrillers, cosmic horror, techno thrillers and dark science fiction, across short films, features, series episodes, games and trailers. The palette is struck strings, bowed metal, hybrid orchestration and custom synthesiser builds, written to picture rather than pulled from a library.

Comedy, romance and straight drama are outside the practice. If your film is not dark, the studio will say so at the first reply rather than three weeks into the schedule.

02How do I start a commission?

Email zaziediya@gmail.com, or use the scoring quote form, with five things: the format (short, feature, series, game or trailer), the scored minutes of music you need, the delivery date or festival deadline, the budget you can spend, and two or three reference films or tracks for tone. A rough cut helps.

After review you receive a written quote: a preliminary estimate — final quotation subject to creative scope and written agreement. Nothing is booked until the scope and payment terms are agreed in writing.

03How many projects run at once?

The studio is a single composer, and scores are written one at a time rather than farmed out to a team. A schedule is confirmed once the scope and payment terms are agreed in writing. A deadline that moves after the schedule is agreed is treated as a change of scope.

04Will you watch a rough cut before quoting?

Yes. Send a link and the studio will watch it before writing the quote, because runtime alone never tells the truth about how much music a film needs. Unreleased material is treated as confidential by default, and a mutual NDA is available on request before anything is sent.

05Do you work remotely, or on site?

Remote by default. Spotting sessions run over video call with the cut playing in sync, revisions move by email and file link, and delivery is electronic. Clients have been scored across several continents this way.

If a production needs the composer in the room for a scoring stage, a dub stage or a session, travel and time are quoted as a separate line.

07What makes the number go up?

Scored minutes and cue count matter most. After that: musical complexity (focused palette, layered hybrid, or bespoke or experimental); schedule (about 10 to 20 days is expedited at plus 15 percent, under ten days is rush at plus 30 percent); revisions beyond the included allowance (one consolidated round for Individual Scene and Focused scores, two for Extended scores; each additional round is {{zp:revision-minimum}} minimum or {{zp:revision-rate}} of the commission fee, whichever is greater); and licence scope. Distribution and exclusive licences cost more, and a copyright buyout or a soundtrack album is quoted individually.

Live players, stems, alternate mixes, surround and other specialist work are not standard, and are quoted separately after a feasibility check. See the full list.

08Are live players included?

No. The base fee covers composition, production, performance on the studio's own instruments and sampled forces, the stereo mix of the cues, and delivery. Where a project wants live soloists or an ensemble, that is budgeted as its own line with the players' fees, and it needs to be raised at quote stage so the writing can be for real instruments rather than retrofitted.

09How is a project paid?

{{zp:payment}} Fees are invoiced in USD. The payment method is named on the invoice. A different split can be agreed in writing, and festival or grant funded productions that need a single invoice on completion should ask before the quote is issued. These terms apply to new commissions only and do not change existing contracts.

Invoices are due within 14 days. Work on an overdue project pauses until the balance is settled, which is a schedule problem for everybody, so it is worth avoiding.

10What happens if my film gets shelved?

If a project is cancelled after work has started, the deposit is retained and any work delivered beyond what the deposit covers is invoiced at the agreed rate. Material already written stays with the studio and may be reused elsewhere unless the agreement says otherwise.

If a project is paused rather than cancelled, the slot is held for 30 days, after which the schedule is rebuilt around whatever capacity exists at that point.

11Who handles tax and withholding?

Fees are quoted net, in USD. If your territory requires withholding on payments to a foreign supplier, that is the production's obligation, and the studio will complete whatever tax form your accounts department needs to avoid it. Sales tax on catalogue purchases is handled by the marketplace that processes the payment.

III. Process and delivery

From spotting session to delivery of the agreed WAV files.

12How long does a score take?

As a rule of thumb, a short takes two to four weeks from spotting to delivery, and a feature takes four to eight depending on scored minutes, cue density and complexity. Games are scheduled against implementation milestones instead of a picture lock.

The date on the quote is the date that matters, and it assumes the picture does not change shape after the spotting session.

13What happens in a spotting session?

The cut plays from head to tail with the director, and every cue is marked: where music starts, where it stops, what it is doing dramatically, and where it must stay out of the way of dialogue or sound design. The output is a cue sheet with timings, a tone agreement per cue, and a delivery schedule.

Spotting is the cheapest place to change your mind. It costs nothing there and a lot later.

14Can I temp with tracks from your showreel?

For internal editing, yes, and it is often useful: tell the studio which cue you cut against and why. Showreel and catalogue material is not licensed for use in a finished picture, a festival cut, a trailer or anything public. If a temp cue has to survive into the film, it is commissioned or licensed in writing.

Temp love is a normal hazard. If you want the score to sound like the temp, say so at spotting. If you want the film to sound like itself, say that instead, and the temp becomes a reference rather than a target.

15How many revisions are included?

Individual Scene commissions include one consolidated revision round. Focused Short-Film scores include one consolidated revision round. Extended Short-Film scores include two consolidated revision rounds. {{zp:revision-definition}} Send each round as one collected set of notes rather than a note a day. Rounds beyond the allowance cost {{zp:revision-minimum}} minimum or {{zp:revision-rate}} of the commission fee per round, whichever is greater, and any recue caused by a picture change after lock is billed as a change order at the rate in your quote.

16What exactly lands in my inbox?

A standard commission is original music composed to picture, delivered as professional stereo WAV files of the agreed cues (24-bit, 48 kHz unless your project specifies otherwise), with a cue list and timings for your edit.

Stems, alternate or clean mixes, surround or immersive mixes, a final film mix, dialogue editing and advanced post-production are specialist work. They can be discussed after a feasibility check and are quoted separately. They are not part of a standard commission.

17What if the picture changes after delivery?

Small trims inside an existing cue are usually absorbed as a courtesy, and the studio will tell you when that is the case. Anything structural, a scene reordered, a sequence dropped, a runtime extended, is a recue and is quoted before it is done.

The fastest route is a new cut with change notes and the previous delivery date you are trying to protect.

18Do you also do sound design?

Sound design, foley and effects are specialist work. They are quoted separately after a feasibility check and are not part of a standard score commission. Ready-made sound libraries are published in the catalogue.

19Is any of it stock or library music?

No. Every commissioned cue is written for your picture, and no cue is generated by a machine model. The composer warrants that the delivered music is original and does not infringe a third party's rights, and any pre existing material the studio owns, its own themes, custom instruments and sample builds, is disclosed in writing and licensed into the deliverables rather than sold.

IV. Rights and credits

Who owns the music, what you may do with it, and how the composer is credited. Full detail lives in licensing, rights and credits.

20Who owns the score?

Composition is a service, and the rights are a separate, written part of each commission. The estimator prices a standard project-use licence as the base: use of the recorded score in the named production, its festival run and its own release, with copyright staying with the composer. Exclusivity, copyright assignment, soundtrack exploitation and expanded uses require a separate written agreement. A copyright buyout, exclusivity, a soundtrack album and extra uses are quoted individually. None of them is added automatically to a low-priced package.

The rights you receive are the ones stated in your written quote. The studio's licensing and terms pages are being reviewed, and they will be updated before any rights position changes.

21Can we release the score as an album?

Not under the standard project-use licence. A commercial soundtrack release is a separate agreement covering the release format, distribution, royalty split and artwork, and it needs to be raised before the score is delivered so the masters are prepared for it.

Festival screenings, press use, clips, trailers and the film's own distribution are covered and need nothing extra.

22How should the composer be credited?

End crawl: Music by Zazie Kanwar-Torge Press and festival listings: Original score by Zazie Kanwar-Torge (Zazie Productions)

Copyable wording, including IMDb and poster variants, is on the licensing page. If a credit cannot be carried in the crawl for a genuine space reason, say so and the studio will agree an alternative placement rather than fight about it.

23Who files the cue sheet?

The production or its distributor files the cue sheet with the performing rights organisations, because it goes with the picture's delivery paperwork. The composer supplies the music side: cue titles, durations, usage type, and his writer and publisher details with his PRO and IPI numbers.

Ask for that data early. It is easier to hand over at delivery than to reconstruct during a distributor's paperwork deadline.

24Can the delivered music be used to train a model?

No. The standard project-use licence does not cover machine learning training on the music, the stems or the session data, and the studio asks for that to stay in the agreement. Equally, no commissioned cue is composed by a generative model.

V. Games and interactive

How a score is written when the picture does not exist yet.

25How does game scoring work?

Game scoring is a separate service with its own process: see game scoring. Layered or adaptive delivery depends on your engine and audio team, so it is scoped and quoted individually after a feasibility check.

26Do game rights work differently?

Game rights are scoped in writing and quoted individually. Game-specific uses, such as updates, ports, re-releases, marketing and player-capture material, need to be named in the agreement. Nothing is assumed by default. If your publishing agreement already has music language, send it with the inquiry.

VI. Catalogue orders

Records, sound libraries, tools and objects. Full detail in purchases, delivery and returns.

27Where does payment happen?

Never on this site. Every card in the catalogue links to the marketplace that hosts the listing, and that marketplace runs the checkout, the payment and the delivery: Bandcamp and itch.io for records and libraries, Gumroad for tools and scores, eBay for physical objects. Their terms apply to the transaction, and this site stores no card data because it never sees any.

28A download link failed or a file is wrong. What now?

Email zaziediya@gmail.com with the order reference from the marketplace and what arrived. Wrong, corrupt or missing files are replaced directly. Digital goods are otherwise final sale, since a download cannot be returned, and physical objects follow the eBay listing's return terms.

29What can I do with the sound libraries?

Use them in your own productions, including commercial ones, without further fee. What you may not do is resell, redistribute or republish the files as a library, or pass them on as part of another sample pack. Each listing states its own licence terms and those govern the purchase; where a listing is silent, the studio will confirm in writing.

VII. Working together

Communication, confidentiality and the things that go wrong on productions.

30How do we communicate during a project?

Email is the record. Notes, approvals, scope changes and delivery dates are confirmed in writing, so nobody has to rely on memory. Replies are sent after review of your material, and the working schedule is confirmed in writing. Calls happen, especially at spotting. Anything decided on a call is written back into the thread the same day.

31Will you sign an NDA?

Yes, a mutual one, and it can be signed before you send a cut. Unreleased material is treated as confidential whether or not paper exists: cuts, scripts and storyboards are not discussed, screened or reused, and project files are stored on encrypted drives and removed after the retention period set out in the privacy notice.

32Can I send a script or a cut unsolicited?

Please do not. An inquiry describing the project is welcome; an unsolicited screenplay, treatment or unfinished cut is not, because the studio develops its own material and cannot accept the risk of a similarity claim later.

If something arrives anyway it will not be read beyond identifying the sender, it creates no obligation and no confidentiality, and the studio remains free to develop anything it has already written or writes independently afterwards.

33What if we disagree about the music?

Most disagreements are about the film rather than the notes, and they resolve at a spotting session with the picture running. The included revision rounds exist for exactly this.

If a production decides the collaboration is not working, it can stop after any delivered stage, pay for what has been delivered, and take the material it has paid for. The terms set out how that is calculated. Nobody benefits from a score finished under protest.

VIII. This website

What the site does on your machine. The full inventory is in the privacy notice.

34Does this site track visitors?

No. There is no analytics package, no advertising pixel and no cookie set by this site, which is why there is no consent banner to dismiss. One browser storage key is written to remember that you have already seen the opening sequence, and a service worker caches pages so repeat visits load instantly.

Video and audio are the exception, because they are hosted elsewhere: no player is requested until you press play, and the catalogue's cover art comes from each marketplace's own image server. The privacy notice lists every host, what it receives and when. The device inspector on that page reads your own browser and shows you what is stored.

35Is the site usable with a screen reader or without JavaScript?

All content on these document pages is in the HTML: with scripting disabled, every answer stays open, every clause is readable, and the section index is a plain list of links. Nothing here depends on a script to appear.

Known gaps and the standard the pages are built to are published in the accessibility statement, along with how to request any document in another format.
