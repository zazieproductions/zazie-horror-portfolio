Glossary: horror scoring and sound design terms

Sixty-seven defined terms covering horror scoring technique, unsettling sound design, delivery formats and the business of commissioning a score. Written for the people who have to name what they want in a spotting session, and for anyone who has to read a composer’s invoice.

  • Compiled by Zazie Kanwar-Torge
  • Scope Scoring, sound design, delivery, rights
  • Companion The Dread Grammar
  • Revised 2026-09-18

IHow to use this glossary

This is a working glossary of the vocabulary used in horror scoring, unsettling sound design and the business of commissioning both. It is written for directors, producers, music supervisors and editors who need to name what they want, and for composers who need a shared reference. Definitions are deliberately plain: where a term has a widely repeated but inaccurate claim attached to it — infrasound is the clearest case — the entry says so.

Terms in italic below cross-reference the Dread Grammar, the named taxonomy of psychological horror scoring techniques published by this studio. Each of the fifteen techniques defined there also appears here as a term, so the vocabulary is consistent across both documents.

IIThe glossary

Acousmatic sound
Sound whose source is deliberately hidden or absent, so the audience hears an event without being able to attach it to a visible cause. Acousmatic material is the backbone of psychological horror scoring because it cannot be resolved by looking, and therefore keeps the audience searching. See also unplaceable source.
Ambisonics
A spatial audio format that captures or renders a full sphere of sound directions rather than a stereo pair, used for immersive and 360-degree horror mixes. In horror work it is most useful for placing a threat behind the audience, which a stereo field cannot do.
Bowed metal
A technique of exciting a metal object—cymbal, saw blade, spring, gong, coil—with a bow rather than a striker, producing sustained, pitch-unstable tones. It is the workhorse of dark atmospheric scoring: it offers pitch, texture and an unplaceable attack in one gesture. See also unplaceable source.
Braam
A dense, brass-and-synth impact used in trailer music to announce genre. Named here because it is the most over-used signifier in horror sound and it converts dread into signalling: an audience that recognises a braam has been told what to feel rather than made unable to predict.
Breath bed
A sustained layer built from recorded human breath, used either as tempo reference or as a low-level presence cue. Because listeners entrain to breath without deciding to, a breath bed changes a viewer's heart rate before it changes their reading of the scene. See breath-led scoring.
Buyout
A rights arrangement in which copyright in the commissioned score transfers to the production, usually as work made for hire or by present assignment. Industry-standard for film work; the composer normally retains the writer's share of performance royalties, credit, and reel and portfolio rights. See licensing and credits.
Cue sheet
The document filed with a performing rights organisation that lists every piece of music in a production, its writer, publisher, duration and usage type. Cue sheets are how composers are paid performance royalties; a score delivered without one leaves money unclaimed.
Convolution reverb
A reverb built by measuring a real space with an impulse response and applying that measurement to new material. For horror it allows the score to inherit the exact acoustics of the location, so the music and the room decay together and the audience cannot separate them. See room as instrument.
Contact microphone
A transducer that picks up vibrations through a solid surface rather than through air. Contact mics turn architecture, furniture and the human body into instruments, and they capture the tactile, internal sound quality that makes horror sound design feel physiological rather than musical.
Diegetic sound
Sound whose source exists inside the fiction: a radio in the room, a band in the bar, a phone. Horror scores exploit the boundary between diegetic and non-diegetic sound by crossing it without a cut. See diegetic contamination.
Drone
A sustained tone or cluster held for a long duration, usually without a melody or a pulse. The drone is the single most economical dread device available: it can hold a scene for minutes at low intensity without fatiguing the audience or competing with dialogue.
Ducking
Automatic reduction of one element's level in response to another, normally music under dialogue. Ducking is a repair, not a technique: if a cue needs heavy ducking to survive under a performance, the arrangement is wrong and the score should be re-voiced rather than turned down.
Fletcher–Munson curves
The standard description of how human hearing changes with level: at low playback volumes, low and high frequencies are perceived as quieter relative to mid-range than the same signal at high volume. This is why a dread mix that is checked quietly can lose its entire sub-bass and sound thin in a living room while sounding enormous in a theatre.
Foley
Performance-recorded sound effects for movement, cloth and contact, created in sync with picture. In horror, foley is also a scoring instrument: a footstep or a hand on fabric can carry the rhythmic role that a percussion part would otherwise take.
Frequency masking
The reduction in audibility of one sound caused by another sharing nearby frequency content. Horror scores are usually dense in the low-mid range, which is exactly where dialogue intelligibility lives, so masking under speech is a scoring problem rather than a mixing problem.
Granular synthesis
A synthesis and processing method that rebuilds sound from thousands of very short fragments, allowing time, pitch and texture to be manipulated independently. It is the standard route to material that is recognisably organic but no longer identifiable. See spectral smearing.
Hybrid orchestral
A scoring approach that combines a recorded ensemble with synthesisers, processed recordings and sound design layers, treating all three as one palette. Most contemporary horror scoring is hybrid, and the practical question is always which layer carries the musical argument and which carries the atmosphere.
Impulse response
A recording of how a specific space responds to a sound, used to place new material in that space. In horror work an impulse response can also be manipulated—stretched, reversed, re-pitched—to produce a space that could not physically exist.
Infrasound
Sound below roughly 20 Hz, at the threshold of human hearing. It is frequently credited with producing unease, fear or nausea in cinema, and those claims are mostly production folklore: commercial cinema systems roll off below about 30 Hz and the reported effects are not reproducible on a screening system. Felt low-frequency pressure in the 30–60 Hz band is what horror scores actually use, and it works.
Just intonation
A tuning system based on whole-number frequency ratios, which produces pure-sounding intervals in a single key at the cost of being unable to move freely between keys. Its purity is audibly different from equal temperament, which makes it a useful colour for folk horror and period material.
Layering
The standard sound-design construction of an event from three roles: a transient that gives the hit, a body that gives the weight, and a tail that gives the space. A single recorded impact rarely does all three, which is why designed horror sounds are almost always layered.
Leitmotif
A recurring musical theme attached to a character, place or idea. Horror rarely uses full leitmotif, because a theme tells the audience who or what matters, and psychological horror depends on the audience not knowing. Horror prefers the recognition loop: something that returns but refuses to mean anything.
LFE channel
The low-frequency effects channel in a surround mix, played through the subwoofer. Because the LFE is a separate channel, material placed there can be felt without occupying the main speakers, which makes it the cleanest route to sub-bass pressure that survives under dialogue.
Limiter
A processor that prevents signal from exceeding a set ceiling. In horror mixing the ceiling matters as much as the average level: if the mix is limited hard, the loudest moment the film has is already spent, and the rupture scene has nowhere to go.
Long decay
Reverb and release times longer than the pictured space could produce, applied gradually enough that the audience never consciously notices the room becoming impossible. A named technique in the Dread Grammar.
LUFS
Loudness Units relative to Full Scale, the measurement used by streaming platforms to normalise playback level. Deliverables for film, television and games each carry their own target, and a score mastered hot for streaming will be turned down and lose its dynamic range on the platform side.
Mid-side processing
A method of separating a signal into its centre (mid) and stereo difference (side) components, so each can be treated independently. It is how a horror score places material to the sides of an audience while keeping dialogue anchored and intelligible in the centre.
Mis-tuned interval
One voice deliberately detuned against a stable one by roughly 10–40 cents, so the ear registers instability before the mind registers a pitch error. A named technique in the Dread Grammar.
M&E stems
Music and Effects: the deliverable in which music and effects are supplied separately from dialogue so a production can re-version the film into another language. Horror scores are usually delivered as separate music stems, effects stems and a combined M&E so a dub can replace the dialogue without touching the score.
Modal interchange
Borrowing chords or material from a parallel mode—most often mixing major and minor—rather than from a distant key. It is the harmonic equivalent of the familiar-but-wrong effect that horror scoring relies on, because it sounds like the same music with something removed.
Mood cluster
A group of cues written to be interchangeable within a stated emotional range, used when a production needs to audition tone without commissioning new material. The showreel at this studio is organised into mood clusters so a director can hear a rate of change rather than a genre.
Musique concrète
A tradition of composition built from recorded real-world sound treated as raw material rather than as documentation, established by Pierre Schaeffer in the late 1940s. Horror sound design is its direct descendant.
Noise floor
The constant low-level sound present in any recording or room: air handling, electrical hum, distant traffic. A room's noise floor is information about the space, and removing it entirely is one of the fastest ways to make a naturalistic scene feel unreal.
Notch filter
A narrow-band equaliser cut used to remove a specific frequency range. Under dialogue it is the standard surgical tool: cutting a narrow band is often enough to keep a dense score audible without turning it down, where a broad cut would remove its body.
Ostinato
A short figure repeated persistently, usually at a fixed pitch level. In horror it functions as a metronome the audience cannot stop hearing, and its power comes from the moment it stops rather than from the moment it begins.
Parallel compression
Mixing a heavily compressed copy of a signal alongside the uncompressed original, preserving transient impact while adding density. It is the standard way to make a sub-bass pressure bed feel enormous without losing the definition that makes it feel physical.
Pedal point
A sustained note held under changing harmony, producing tension between the fixed pitch and the movement above it. It is the cheapest available way to make simple material feel unstable, and it survives on small speakers that reproduce nothing below 100 Hz.
Phase cancellation
Loss of signal where two similar waveforms meet out of phase. In horror scoring it is used deliberately for the hollow, physically uncomfortable quality it produces, and accidentally whenever low-frequency material is collapsed to mono for a television or phone speaker.
Portamento
A continuous glide between pitches rather than a jump. The slow downward glide—variously heard as a sag, a sigh or a failing mechanism—is one of the most reliable signifiers of something going wrong in a horror cue.
Prepared piano
A piano with objects placed on or between its strings to alter timbre and decay, established by John Cage. It supplies a horror palette that is familiar in pitch but wrong in physical behaviour, which is why it reads as domestic space that has stopped working.
Pressure bed
A sustained low-frequency layer whose function is felt rather than heard, delivered as its own stem so a re-recording mixer can place it under dialogue. See sub-bass pressure.
Recognition loop
A returning figure that degrades slightly at every statement, so repetition itself becomes the source of unease. A named technique in the Dread Grammar.
Reverse reverb
A reverb whose tail precedes the sound that causes it, produced by reversing the material before and after processing. Because the cause arrives after the effect, the ear cannot assign causality, which produces anticipation without an event.
Room tone
The ambient sound of a location recorded without dialogue or action, used to fill the gaps between takes. In horror, room tone is also a creative asset: the vacuum technique works by removing the score and letting the audience hear the room instead.
Score stem
An isolated deliverable containing one portion of the mix—strings, synthesis, pressure, percussion—rather than the whole score. Stems let a production re-mix, re-version or remove part of a score without going back to the composer, and are standard on any professional delivery.
Shepard tone
An auditory illusion built from overlapping octaves that appears to rise or fall forever without ever arriving. It is the most literal musical expression of dread available, and it is best used once, briefly, because the illusion is easily recognised.
Silence budget
The allocation of non-musical time across a film as a scored element, agreed before writing begins. A named technique in the Dread Grammar.
Soundalike
Newly written music designed to sit close to an existing commercial track in style and feel without reproducing it. A legitimate and common brief; the boundary to avoid is reproducing the actual melodic, harmonic or recorded content of the referenced work.
Spectral smearing
Time-stretching, blurring and convolving a recording until its transients are gone, so an event reads as a condition rather than an action. A named technique in the Dread Grammar.
Spotting session
The meeting where the director, composer and usually the picture editor walk the film and decide where music starts, where it stops, and what each cue has to do. On this studio's commissions, spotting is where the dread ladder and the silence budget are agreed in writing.
Stinger
A short, loud, typically orchestral or percussive hit synchronised to a shock. Stingers are punctuation: used early and often, they teach the audience that loudness is the score's only register, and they spend the top of the dynamic range before the film has earned it.
Sub-bass
Frequencies below roughly 60 Hz, felt as pressure as much as heard as pitch. The most reliable dread tool in scoring, and the first casualty on phone and laptop speakers, which is why it is usually paired with a mid-range texture carrying the same rhythm. See sub-bass pressure.
Sub-drop
The sudden removal of low-frequency energy, usually immediately before an impact. It is the inverse of a stinger: instead of adding an event, it removes a floor, so anything that follows arrives with greater weight.
Sustained tone
A single continuous pitch held without articulation. Because it provides no rhythmic information and no event, it can hold a scene indefinitely at low intensity without drawing attention—the defining characteristic of the presence rung. See the dread ladder.
Tape saturation
Harmonic distortion produced by driving analogue tape or its emulation. In horror, mild saturation thickens low material so it is felt on small speakers, and heavy saturation gives a source the degraded, unreliable quality associated with found footage and memory.
Temp score
Temporary music placed in the edit before the score exists, to judge pacing. Also called temp love when a director becomes attached to it. A temp track states a genre, rarely a rate of change, so the useful question about it is what it does to the scene rather than what it sounds like. See what the Dread Grammar refuses.
Temperament drift
Moving between equal temperament and another tuning system inside a single cue, so the ear loses its reference for what is in tune. A named technique in the Dread Grammar.
Theremin
An early electronic instrument played without contact, producing a continuous pitch that is notoriously hard to fix precisely. Its imprecision is its value in horror: it produces a human-sounding line that will not settle on a note. Widely associated with mid-century science fiction and supernatural scores.
Tone cluster
A group of adjacent pitches sounded together, traditionally with a forearm on a piano. The cluster is the most recognisable horror signifier in existence and therefore the weakest: it tells the audience which kind of film they are watching rather than making them unable to predict what happens next.
Transient
The initial, high-energy portion of a sound that carries its sense of impact and its causality. Removing transients converts a hit into a condition, which is the mechanism behind several dread techniques; preserving them is what makes a score feel like it is punctuating the picture.
Tritone
The interval of six semitones, historically called the devil's interval. In practice it is a strong, stable dissonance rather than an intrinsically frightening sound, and its reputation does more work than its musical content. Useful as a colour, weak as a strategy.
Underscore
Music written to sit beneath dialogue and action, designed to be felt rather than listened to. Most of any horror feature's score is underscore, and its success is usually measured by whether the audience noticed it at all.
Unplaceable source
A sound with no attack transient, no identifiable material and no room signature, so the listener cannot determine its source, distance or size. A named technique in the Dread Grammar.
Vacuum
The removal of the score at the moment of maximum anxiety, leaving production sound and the audience's own anticipation. The cheapest rung of the dread ladder and the one most often lost between spotting and mix.
Waterphone
A steel bowl with tuned rods and a water-filled chamber, played by bowing or striking. It produces a shimmering, pitch-unstable tone with an unusually long tail, which is why it became a standard texture in supernatural and psychological horror scoring from the 1970s onward.
White noise, pink noise and brown noise
Broadband noise characterised by how energy is distributed across frequency: white is equal per frequency, pink falls at 3 dB per octave, brown falls at 6 dB and concentrates energy in the low end. Brown noise is the one used in horror, because its balance sits where the body feels pressure, and because it masks the mid-range detail that the ear uses to identify a source.
Work made for hire
A copyright doctrine under which work created within the scope of a commission belongs to the commissioning party from the moment of creation. It is the cleanest basis for a film production to own a score outright, and it is why the studio's default rights model is a buyout with the composer retaining the writer's share. IIIQuestions about the vocabulary

01What is a cue sheet in film scoring?

A cue sheet is the document filed with a performing rights organisation that lists every piece of music in a production — its writer, publisher, duration and usage type. It is how composers and publishers are paid performance royalties when a film is broadcast or streamed, so a score delivered without one leaves money unclaimed. On this studio’s commissions the cue sheet is prepared as part of delivery.

02What is the difference between a score and a soundtrack?

A score is the original music written for the picture. A soundtrack is a released album, which may contain the score, licensed songs, or both. The distinction matters commercially: a soundtrack release is a separate licence from the film score commission, which is why the studio treats album rights as a separate agreement from the buyout of the commissioned score.

03What does non-diegetic mean?

Non-diegetic music is sound the characters cannot hear — score that exists for the audience only. Diegetic music has a source inside the fiction, such as a radio or a band in a bar. Horror scores get much of their unease from crossing that boundary deliberately, so the audience stops being able to tell when the film has started scoring them.

04What is a spotting session?

A spotting session is the meeting where the director, composer and usually the picture editor walk the film and decide where music starts, where it stops, and what each cue has to do. It is the single most important meeting in a commission, because decisions taken there — especially where the score stops — are what the rest of the schedule is built on.

05What is LUFS and why does it matter for a horror score?

LUFS measures perceived loudness on a scale where streaming platforms normalise playback. Each delivery target — cinema, broadcast, streaming, game — has its own expected range. A horror score mastered hot for streaming gets turned down by the platform, which flattens exactly the dynamic contrast that dread depends on.
